The competition in one paragraph

A subject (an AI system) is evaluated on a benchmark of items (tasks). For each subject–benchmark pair, some item outcomes are hidden. You may reveal a small number of outcomes from a separate acquisition pool; the budgets are \(B \in \{0,1,3,7,15,31\}\). At every budget you must output a probability \(p_{sj}\) that subject \(s\) succeeds on evaluation item \(j\). The scorer compares those probabilities with the hidden binary outcomes using the Brier score, then averages across budgets with fixed weights. Nothing else is scored. Your job is to turn content (item text, metadata, subject metadata) plus a few revealed labels into honest probabilities for items you have never seen the outcome of.

The official flow. Anything that uses the hidden evaluation outcomes, directly or indirectly, is leakage — even if it “only” tunes a calibration constant.

Five objects, one sentence each

Subjectthe AI system being evaluated — a model, an agent, or a model plus tooling.
Itemone task presented to the subject (a coding issue, a math problem, a web task).
Outcomewhat actually happened, encoded as \(y \in \{0,1\}\).
Predictoryour submitted program; it estimates outcomes without running the subject.
Predictiona probability \(p \in [0,1]\); not a label, not a decision, not a ranking.

The subject is not your predictor. Your predictor predicts how the subject will perform.

Three questions every method must answer

  1. Item side: where do difficulty and discrimination come from? A learned table, item text, metadata, or an amortized network \(f_\phi(x_j)\)?
  2. Subject side: where does ability come from? A learned table, subject metadata, or the revealed labels?
  3. Update rule: exactly how do the revealed labels change the prediction? A posterior, a MAP fit, a learned set encoder, or nothing?

If you can answer those three for any model in the repo, you understand it. The rest is bookkeeping.

Reference · five minutes

Math survival kit

Nine things from high-school math that the rest of this guide assumes. Each comes with the smallest possible example.

1 · Sums, products, and the two Greek letters

\(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i = x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n\) adds; \(\prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i = p_1p_2\cdots p_n\) multiplies. A likelihood over independent observations is a product, which is why we take logs immediately.

Solved

For \(x=(1,2,3)\): \(\sum x_i = 6\), \(\prod x_i = 6\), and \(\sum x_i^2 = 14\). The mean is \(6/3=2\).

2 · Exponentials and logarithms

\(\log\) undoes \(e^{(\cdot)}\). The three rules you will use constantly:

\[ \log(ab)=\log a+\log b,\qquad \log(a/b)=\log a-\log b,\qquad \log(a^b)=b\log a. \]

Probability lives in \((0,1)\); log-probability lives in \((-\infty,0]\). That unbounded scale is why we model logits instead of probabilities.

Solved

\(\log(0.8\cdot 0.3)=\log 0.8+\log 0.3=-0.223-1.204=-1.427\). And \(e^{-1.427}=0.24=0.8\cdot0.3\).

3 · Derivatives and the chain rule

\(\frac{d}{dx}x^2=2x\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\log x=1/x\), \(\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x\). The chain rule: if \(L=L(f(x))\), then \(dL/dx=(dL/df)(df/dx)\). Every “gradient through the network” is only this, repeated.

The sigmoid has a derivative worth memorizing:

\[ \sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr). \]
Solved

At \(z=0\), \(\sigma=0.5\), so \(\sigma'=0.25\). At \(z=2\), \(\sigma=0.881\), so \(\sigma'=0.881\cdot0.119=0.105\). The curve is steepest at zero.

4 · Probability rules

For events \(A,B\): \(P(A,B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)\). If independent, \(P(A,B)=P(A)P(B)\). Bayes flips the conditioning:

\[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}. \]

In this guide, \(A\) is “the parameters” and \(B\) is “the labels we saw”. The denominator is a normalizing constant; the interesting part is \(P(B\mid A)P(A)\).

5 · Expectation and variance

For a discrete random variable \(X\): \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_x x\,P(X=x)\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}X)^2\).

For a Bernoulli outcome with success probability \(p\): \(\mathbb{E}[Y]=p\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=p(1-p)\). The variance is largest at \(p=0.5\) and zero at the extremes.

Solved

Six outcomes \((1,0,1,1,0,1)\): mean \(4/6=0.667\). Sample variance \(=0.267\), which equals \(p(1-p)\cdot n/(n-1)=0.222\cdot1.2\).

6 · The Gaussian

\(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\) has density \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\). About 68% of mass lies within one standard deviation, 95% within two. A variance of 4 means a standard deviation of 2 — the units matter.

7 · Vectors, dot products, matrices

A dot product \(w^\top x=\sum_i w_ix_i\) is a weighted sum; it is also a similarity score when both vectors are normalized. A weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) maps an \(n\)-vector to an \(m\)-vector: \(y=Wx+b\). Neural networks are stacks of these with a nonlinearity between them.

8 · Softmax, argmax, and “max”

Softmax turns arbitrary scores \(s_i\) into positive numbers that sum to one: \(\operatorname{softmax}(s)_i=e^{s_i}/\sum_k e^{s_k}\). Argmax returns the index of the largest value; \(\max\) returns the value.

Solved

Scores \((0,1,2)\): softmax \(=(0.090,\,0.245,\,0.665)\). The argmax is index 3; the max is 2. Attention is a softmax over context items.

9 · Reading an equation without panic

  1. Name every symbol. If one is unnamed, the equation is not yet understood.
  2. Check the output type. Probability? Real number? Vector? Distribution?
  3. Plug in extremes. Set \(p=0\), \(p=1\), \(\theta=0\), \(n=0\). Does the formula still make sense?
  4. Check monotonicity. If ability rises, should the probability rise? Does the formula do that?
  5. Find the units. A logit is dimensionless; a Brier score is squared probability; a KL is in nats.
Intuition

Almost every model in this competition is the same sentence in different clothing: a subject’s ability, compared with an item’s difficulty, squashed into a probability. The Neural Process changes only where ability and difficulty come from, and how labels update them.
